Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Alisha Wang, Calvin Chen, Ziyan Liu — alw054@ucsd.edu, cac028@ucsd.edu, zil117@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Aviation is becoming more sustainable ✈️♻️

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Decision 1 [Score: -1]: We chose to plot carbon intensity instead of absolute CO2 emissions. This framing makes aviation appear more sustainable because it only measures efficiency, not quantity. When the audience looks at the graph and sees that the distribution has shifted left, they would infer that there are lower carbon emissions, but it only says there are emissions per dollar spent. The graph doesn't account for the increase in flights over time, which has led to higher emissions from the aviation industry as a whole. In other words, while the graph is technically accurate, it prompts the reader to draw the wrong conclusions.
  • Decision 2 [Score: -1.5]: The visualization gives each country equal importance, which can be misleading because a small country with limited aviation activity counts just as much as a major aviation market. This means the chart treats each country like one equal vote, regardless of how much flying it actually does. As a result, the improvement can look larger than it really is, since weighting by passenger volume would likely show a smaller decline in carbon intensity, especially because high-growth markets like China and Southeast Asia improved less.
  • Decision 3 [Score: -2]: The title says 'CO2 Emissions from Aviation Have Decreased by ~30%', and below it in smaller font and lighter color, we explain that it's for 'per dollar of aviation output, for the median country'. By phrasing the big title in simple language, we highlight that emissions have decreased, prompting the audience to believe that aviation has become more sustainable. While we do explain the units and measure of central tendency in the subtitles, it's much less emphasized, and the reader may skim over it without truly understanding what it means. We also rounded the 29.0% to ~30% to make the decrease feel more significant, without risking the reader easily noticing the 1% difference.
  • Decision 4 [Score: -0.5]: The use of a smoothed KDE curve can hide details in the data. By smoothing the distribution, extreme values and country-to-country variation become less visible, making the change from 1995 to 2018 look cleaner and more uniform than the raw data may actually show.
  • Decision 5 [Score: -0.5]: The bright blue 2018 curve makes the newer distribution stand out more than the 1995 one. This visual emphasis pushes the viewer to focus on the apparent improvement and makes the 2018 pattern feel more important than the 1995 curve.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Decision 1 [Score: +2]: We used a line plot because position along a common scale is one of the most accurate visual encodings for showing changes over time. This allows viewers to easily perceive the upward trend and compare values across years.
  • Decision 2 [Score: -1]: We combined a pun-based headline with a plane icon to reinforce the idea of emissions “taking off.” This creates a strong visual and linguistic metaphor that makes the message more engaging and memorable, but also subtly biases viewers toward interpreting the trend as rapid growth.
  • Decision 3 [Score: -0.5]: We used a bold red color for the emissions line and annotation to draw immediate attention to the upward trend. Red leverages pre-attentive processing and emphasizes increase, making the key pattern easy to spot. However, it can also bias perception toward urgency or severity.
  • Decision 4 [Score: +1.5]: We summed country-level CO₂ emissions into a single yearly total to create a clear and interpretable time series. This simplifies the dataset and highlights the overall trend effectively. However, it removes variation across countries and hides regional differences. We considered plotting multiple lines, but chose aggregation for clarity and focus.

Final Reflection

Working on these two visualizations helped me see how the same dataset can tell very different stories depending on the choices we make. The anti-proposition visualization was easier to build because the message was more direct. Aviation CO₂ emissions increased over time. Using total emissions made the upward trend clear right away, and adding one main line, a trend line, and an annotation made the argument feel very persuasive. The pro-proposition visualization was harder because it required a different metric. Instead of total emissions, we used carbon intensity, which shows emissions compared to economic output. This supports the idea that aviation became more efficient, but it is less obvious and needs more explanation. Showing multiple regions also made the chart more visually complex for the viewers. What surprised us most was that both charts can be technically true, but they guide the viewer toward very different conclusions.

This project changed how we think about ethical analysis and visualization. We do not think a visualization can ever be completely neutral because every chart involves choices about what to include, what to leave out, and how to present the data. To us, ethical visualization means being honest about those choices. It should make the data understandable without hiding important context or making the pattern look stronger than it really is. Since people often trust charts quickly, even small design decisions can shape how they interpret the issue.

We think persuasive choices are acceptable when they highlight a real pattern in the data and are clearly labeled and supported. For example, choosing carbon intensity or total emissions is acceptable as long as the chart explains what is being measured. However, a choice becomes misleading when it over exaggerates the data and removes important context, leading to the viewer believing something the data does not fully support. We now see ethical visualization as a balance between displaying a clear story or message and being fair to the data.
